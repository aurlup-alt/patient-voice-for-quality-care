# PREMs Measurement Guide

## 1. Introduction

Patient-Reported Experience Measures (PREMs) are tools used to capture patients' reports of their experiences while receiving healthcare services.

They help healthcare organisations understand how patients experience different aspects of care, including access, communication, respect, involvement in decisions, coordination, responsiveness, and continuity of care.

Clinical outcomes and service statistics are important, but they do not always explain how patients experience the care they receive. PREMs provide a complementary perspective that can help organisations identify service gaps and prioritise improvement.

This guide provides a practical framework for planning, collecting, analysing, interpreting, and using PREMs data in healthcare quality improvement.

**Intended audience:** Healthcare quality improvement teams, hospital administrators, health system leaders, patient safety professionals, researchers, and patient engagement teams.

## 2. Objectives of PREMs Measurement

A PREMs measurement initiative should aim to:

- Understand patients' experiences of healthcare services.
- Identify strengths and gaps in the delivery of care.
- Monitor patient experience over time.
- Support evidence-informed quality improvement.
- Strengthen patient-centred care and organisational accountability.
- Assess whether improvement interventions lead to better reported experiences.

Measurement should have a clear purpose. Collecting patient feedback without analysing it or acting on the findings may create unnecessary burden for patients and staff.

## 3. PREMs and PROMs: Understanding the Difference

| Aspect | PREMs | PROMs |
|---|---|---|
| Full term | Patient-Reported Experience Measures | Patient-Reported Outcome Measures |
| Main focus | Patients' experiences of care | Patients' health status and outcomes as reported by patients |
| Example question | Were you involved in decisions about your treatment? | How much has your pain affected your daily activities? |
| Typical improvement use | Communication, access, dignity, coordination, responsiveness | Symptoms, functioning, health-related quality of life |
| Relationship | Explains aspects of how care is experienced | Describes aspects of how patients feel or function |

PREMs and PROMs are complementary. Neither replaces clinical outcome measures, patient safety data, or other quality indicators.

## 4. Step 1: Define the Measurement Purpose

Before selecting a questionnaire, answer the following questions:

1. What patient experience problem are we trying to understand?
2. Which healthcare service or patient group is involved?
3. How will the findings be used?
4. Who will review the results?
5. What decisions or improvement actions could follow?

### Example

**Problem:** Patients attending an outpatient department report that they are not adequately informed about waiting times.

**Measurement purpose:** Assess patients' experience of waiting-time communication and identify opportunities to improve information provision.

**Potential action:** Test a process for communicating expected waiting times and measure whether patients' reported experiences improve.

A specific purpose helps keep the survey relevant and limits unnecessary data collection.

## 5. Step 2: Select the Appropriate Instrument

Where possible, use a validated PREMs instrument that is appropriate for the healthcare setting, population, language, and intended purpose.

Before selecting an instrument, assess:

- Relevance to the service and patient population.
- Evidence of validity and reliability.
- Availability of appropriate language versions.
- Cultural and contextual suitability.
- Length and respondent burden.
- Accessibility for people with disabilities or communication needs.
- Permission, licensing, and conditions of use.
- Suitability for monitoring change or comparing services.

Do not assume that an instrument validated in one country or setting is automatically valid in another.

If no suitable instrument is available, a locally developed questionnaire may be considered. It should be clearly identified as locally developed and tested before routine use.

## 6. Step 3: Define the Domains to Be Measured

The following domains may be considered according to the purpose of the measurement initiative.

| Domain | Example measurement focus |
|---|---|
| Access to care | Ease of obtaining the required service |
| Waiting time | Experience of delays and waiting-time information |
| Communication | Clarity and understandability of explanations |
| Respect and dignity | Respectful treatment and preservation of dignity |
| Involvement in decisions | Opportunity to participate in care decisions |
| Privacy and confidentiality | Perceived protection of privacy |
| Responsiveness | Staff response when help is needed |
| Coordination and continuity | Information sharing and continuity between services |
| Discharge and follow-up | Understanding of care plans and next steps |
| Overall experience | Patient's overall assessment of the service |

Not every project needs to measure every domain. Select domains that are relevant to the improvement question.

## 7. Step 4: Develop an Operational Measurement Plan

Document how data will be collected and analysed before starting.

| Measurement element | Questions to address |
|---|---|
| Population | Which patients are eligible? |
| Sampling | How will participants be selected? |
| Setting | Where and when will feedback be collected? |
| Method | Paper, interview, telephone, digital, or another approach? |
| Timing | During care, at discharge, or after the encounter? |
| Frequency | One-time assessment or repeated measurement? |
| Responsibility | Who will collect and review the data? |
| Data protection | How will confidentiality and secure handling be maintained? |
| Analysis | Which indicators and response categories will be reported? |
| Action | Who will review findings and decide on improvement actions? |

Use a consistent method where the objective is to monitor change over time.

## 8. Step 5: Plan Sampling and Participation

The sampling strategy should reflect the purpose and practical constraints of the initiative.

Possible approaches include:

- **Census:** Invite all eligible patients during a defined period.
- **Random sampling:** Select patients randomly from the eligible population.
- **Systematic sampling:** Invite patients using a defined interval, such as every fifth eligible patient.
- **Convenience sampling:** Invite patients who are readily available; this may be practical for local learning but is more vulnerable to selection bias.

### Practical considerations

- Define inclusion and exclusion criteria in advance.
- Consider different patient groups, service types, and times of day.
- Avoid systematically excluding patients with language, literacy, disability, or digital-access barriers.
- Record the number of eligible patients approached and the number who respond, where feasible.
- Report the sampling method and important limitations.

There is no single sample size suitable for every PREMs initiative. Sample size depends on the intended decision, expected response rate, desired precision, population size, and whether comparisons are planned. Formal research or benchmarking may require statistical advice and a prespecified sample size calculation.

## 9. Step 6: Collect Data Ethically and Consistently

Patient feedback should be collected in a way that encourages honest responses and protects participants.

Recommended practices include:

- Explain the purpose of feedback collection.
- Make participation voluntary and avoid coercion.
- Clarify that participation or non-participation will not affect access to care.
- Provide accessible and understandable questions.
- Avoid leading questions and pressure to provide positive ratings.
- Protect privacy during completion or interviews.
- Collect only the information necessary for the stated purpose.
- Follow applicable consent, ethics review, confidentiality, and data protection requirements.

Consider whether patients may feel uncomfortable criticising staff if staff members collect feedback directly. Independent collection or confidential response methods may reduce this risk.

## 10. Step 7: Define PREMs Indicators

Indicators should be clearly defined so that results can be interpreted consistently.

### Indicator A: Positive Experience Percentage

**Purpose:** Measure the proportion of valid responses that fall within a predefined positive response category.

**Example definition:** Percentage of valid responses rated 4 or 5 on a five-point scale, where 4 and 5 have been defined in advance as positive responses.

**Formula:**

Positive experience (%) = (Number of valid responses rated 4 or 5 ÷ Total valid responses to the item) × 100

Exclude missing and not-applicable responses from the denominator. Report the number of valid responses alongside the percentage.

### Indicator B: Survey Response Rate

**Purpose:** Describe participation among eligible patients who were invited to respond.

**Formula:**

Response rate (%) = (Number of completed surveys ÷ Number of eligible patients invited) × 100

Define what counts as a completed survey and apply the definition consistently. If eligibility or invitation numbers cannot be reliably established, report the available participation information without presenting an inaccurate response rate.

A high response rate does not, by itself, prove that the results are representative.

### Indicator C: Communication Experience

**Purpose:** Monitor patients' reported experience of communication.

**Example definition:** Percentage of valid responses indicating that explanations were clear and understandable, using a prespecified response threshold.

**Possible improvement action:** Introduce a structured communication approach and assess whether reported experience improves.

### Indicator D: Involvement in Care Decisions

**Purpose:** Assess whether patients experienced the level of involvement in decisions that they wanted.

**Example definition:** Percentage of valid responses indicating that patients were involved in decisions to the extent they wanted.

Interpret results in the context of patient preferences and the nature of the clinical decision.

### Indicator E: Respect and Dignity

**Purpose:** Monitor patients' reported experience of respectful treatment and preservation of dignity.

**Example definition:** Percentage of valid responses indicating a positive experience of respect and dignity, according to a predefined scoring rule.

### Important measurement note

The definitions above are illustrative. They are not universal standards or validated benchmarks. Use the scoring rules recommended for a validated instrument when one is selected. Do not change validated scoring rules without documenting the change and understanding its implications.

## 11. Step 8: Analyse and Interpret the Results

### Quantitative analysis

Depending on the instrument and purpose, analysis may include:

- Number of respondents and valid responses per item.
- Response distributions for each question.
- Percentages in predefined response categories.
- Median or mean scores where appropriate.
- Missing and not-applicable response rates.
- Differences across service areas or time periods, where comparisons are valid.

For ordinal response scales, response distributions and medians may be more informative than means alone. Follow the scoring guidance of the selected instrument.

### Qualitative analysis

Open-ended comments may provide context for numerical results.

A practical process is to:

1. Review comments systematically.
2. Develop a set of recurring themes.
3. Code responses consistently.
4. Identify common concerns and positive experiences.
5. Examine differences across relevant groups where appropriate.
6. Select de-identified examples that illustrate findings without exposing individuals.

Do not treat the number of comments about an issue as a direct estimate of how common the issue is among all patients.

### Interpretation questions

- Which aspects of the patient experience appear strongest?
- Which aspects require improvement?
- Are particular groups or services reporting different experiences?
- Could the findings be affected by sampling or response bias?
- Do comments help explain the quantitative findings?
- What additional information is needed before taking action?

## 12. Step 9: Monitor Patient Experience Over Time

Repeated measurement can help teams understand whether changes are associated with improved patient experience.

When monitoring trends:

- Use consistent question wording and scoring.
- Keep the sampling approach and collection method as consistent as practical.
- Document changes in the questionnaire, population, or survey process.
- Display results over time when enough observations are available.
- Interpret changes in light of sample sizes and uncertainty.
- Avoid claiming that an intervention caused an improvement without an appropriate evaluation design.

For local improvement work, a run chart may help teams visualise patient experience results over time. Use a clearly defined measure and a consistent reporting interval.

## 13. Step 10: Translate Findings into Improvement

Measurement has value when findings inform decisions and actions.

Use the following improvement cycle:

1. **Listen:** Collect patient-reported experiences.
2. **Understand:** Analyse results and explore the underlying issues.
3. **Prioritise:** Select an important, actionable problem.
4. **Co-design:** Involve patients or representatives and staff in developing a response where feasible.
5. **Test:** Use a small-scale PDSA cycle to test a change.
6. **Measure:** Monitor patient experience and relevant process or balancing measures.
7. **Learn:** Review results and adapt the intervention.
8. **Communicate:** Explain to patients and staff what has changed and what remains to be done.

### Example: Improving communication about waiting times

**Baseline measure:** Percentage of valid responses rating waiting-time communication positively.

**Intervention:** Introduce a standard process for providing waiting-time updates.

**Process measure:** Percentage of sampled encounters in which an update was provided according to the agreed process.

**Outcome measure:** Percentage of valid patient responses indicating a positive experience of waiting-time communication.

**Balancing measure:** Staff workload or disruption to other service activities.

The team should define the measurement period, data collection method, baseline, and locally appropriate target before testing the intervention.

## 15. Common Pitfalls to Avoid

- Collecting feedback without a clear purpose.
- Using untested questions as if they were validated instruments.
- Surveying only patients who are easiest to reach.
- Pressuring patients to provide positive feedback.
- Ignoring missing responses and sampling limitations.
- Comparing scores obtained using materially different methods.
- Treating overall satisfaction as a complete measure of care quality.
- Collecting identifiable information unnecessarily.
- Failing to communicate actions taken in response to feedback.
- Making claims about improvement without examining the evidence.

## 16. Ethical and Governance Considerations

PREMs measurement should be proportionate to its purpose and comply with applicable organisational policies and legal requirements.

Distinguish routine service improvement from formal research, evaluation, or benchmarking where relevant. Obtain appropriate ethics review, permissions, or other approvals when required.

Patient comments may contain sensitive information even when names are not requested. Apply appropriate access controls, secure storage, retention rules, and de-identification practices.

## 17. Suggested Implementation Checklist

- [ ] A clear measurement purpose has been defined.
- [ ] The patient population and service have been specified.
- [ ] An appropriate instrument has been selected or a local tool has been pilot-tested.
- [ ] Questions and response options are understandable and accessible.
- [ ] Sampling and data collection methods are documented.
- [ ] Privacy and data protection arrangements are in place.
- [ ] Indicators and scoring rules are defined before analysis.
- [ ] The analysis plan accounts for missing responses and limitations.
- [ ] A team is responsible for reviewing the findings.
- [ ] Improvement actions and follow-up measures are identified.
- [ ] Patients and staff will be informed about actions taken.

## Conclusion

PREMs provide an important perspective on healthcare quality by making patients' reported experiences visible to healthcare teams and leaders.

A meaningful PREMs initiative goes beyond collecting scores. It uses appropriate measurement methods, interprets findings responsibly, involves patients in improvement, and communicates the actions taken.

**Listen to patients. Measure what matters. Improve care.**
