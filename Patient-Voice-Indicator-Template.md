# Patient Voice Indicator Template

## 1. Purpose

This template helps healthcare organisations define, measure, analyse, and monitor indicators that capture patients' experiences, perspectives, and involvement in care.

Patient voice indicators should support learning and improvement, not merely data collection or performance reporting. Each indicator should lead to meaningful action that improves patient-centred care.

This template can be adapted for hospitals, clinics, primary healthcare facilities, outpatient departments, and other healthcare services.

## 2. How to Use This Template

For each indicator:

1. Identify the patient experience or care process that needs improvement.
2. Define exactly what will be measured.
3. Specify the numerator, denominator, and calculation method where applicable.
4. Identify the data source and collection method.
5. Establish a baseline before setting a target.
6. Assign responsibility for data collection, analysis, and action.
7. Review results regularly and use findings to guide improvement.
8. Reassess whether changes have improved patients' experiences.

Use validated patient-reported experience measures (PREMs) when appropriate. Locally developed questions should be pilot-tested before being used for formal performance comparisons.

## 4. Example Patient Voice Indicators

The following examples are starting points, not universal standards. Adapt the definitions and response options to the local context.

### Indicator 1: Positive Communication Experience

**Purpose:** Assess whether patients felt that healthcare professionals explained information clearly.

- **Numerator:** Number of respondents who selected a predefined positive response to the communication question.
- **Denominator:** Number of eligible respondents who answered that question.
- **Formula:** Positive communication responses / All valid responses × 100.
- **Data source:** Patient experience survey.
- **Suggested question:** "Did healthcare professionals explain your condition, treatment, or care plan in a way you could understand?"
- **Review frequency:** Monthly, or according to the volume of responses.
- **Potential improvement action:** Test plain-language explanations, teach-back, or improved patient information materials.

### Indicator 2: Respect and Dignity

**Purpose:** Assess whether patients felt treated respectfully.

- **Numerator:** Number of respondents reporting that they were treated with respect and dignity according to the predefined positive-response category.
- **Denominator:** Number of eligible respondents who answered the question.
- **Formula:** Positive respect-and-dignity responses / All valid responses × 100.
- **Data source:** Patient survey, interviews, or patient feedback.
- **Suggested question:** "Were you treated with respect and dignity throughout your care?"
- **Potential improvement action:** Review staff communication practices, privacy arrangements, and patient concerns.

### Indicator 3: Involvement in Care Decisions

**Purpose:** Assess whether patients felt involved in decisions about their care.

- **Numerator:** Number of respondents who reported the expected level of involvement.
- **Denominator:** Number of eligible respondents who answered the question.
- **Formula:** Responses meeting the predefined involvement criterion / All valid responses × 100.
- **Suggested question:** "Were you involved as much as you wanted to be in decisions about your care?"
- **Potential improvement action:** Improve shared decision-making, explanation of options, and opportunities to ask questions.

The expected level of involvement should reflect patient preferences; not every patient wants the same degree of participation.

### Indicator 4: Communication About Waiting Time

**Purpose:** Assess whether patients received understandable information about delays.

- **Numerator:** Number of respondents who experienced a delay and reported receiving adequate information about it.
- **Denominator:** Number of eligible respondents who experienced a delay and answered the question.
- **Formula:** Patients adequately informed about delays / Eligible respondents experiencing delays × 100.
- **Suggested question:** "If your care was delayed, were you given clear information about the reason for the delay or the expected waiting time?"
- **Potential improvement action:** Introduce regular delay updates and a clear process for communicating changes.

Patients who did not experience a delay should not be included in this indicator's denominator.

### Indicator 5: Patient Feedback Response Rate

**Purpose:** Monitor participation in patient feedback collection.

- **Numerator:** Number of eligible patients who submitted a usable response.
- **Denominator:** Number of eligible patients invited to participate.
- **Formula:** Usable responses / Eligible patients invited × 100.
- **Data source:** Survey distribution and response records.
- **Potential improvement action:** Review survey length, accessibility, language options, and the timing of invitations.

A high response rate does not automatically mean that the feedback represents all patients. Assess non-response and potential sampling bias.

### Indicator 6: Patient Feedback Action Closure

**Purpose:** Assess whether agreed actions arising from patient feedback are completed.

- **Numerator:** Number of patient-feedback improvement actions completed and verified by the review date.
- **Denominator:** Number of patient-feedback improvement actions due for completion by that date.
- **Formula:** Completed and verified actions due / All actions due × 100.
- **Data source:** Patient feedback action log.
- **Review frequency:** Monthly or at the scheduled quality review.
- **Potential improvement action:** Assign action owners, deadlines, and verification criteria.

Completion of an action does not prove that patient experience improved. Where possible, evaluate the effect of the change using a relevant patient voice indicator.

## 7. Using Indicators for Quality Improvement

Patient voice indicators should be integrated into a structured improvement process.

### Step 1: Identify the problem

Review survey findings, patient comments, complaints, interviews, and other relevant sources.

### Step 2: Understand the causes

Discuss the findings with patients and frontline staff. Explore workflow, communication, accessibility, organisational culture, and other potential contributing factors.

### Step 3: Select a change

Choose a specific, feasible intervention that addresses a plausible cause of the problem.

### Step 4: Test the change

Use Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycles to test the change on a small scale and learn from the results.

### Step 5: Measure the effect

Monitor the relevant patient voice indicator before and after the intervention. Use consistent definitions and collection methods where possible.

### Step 6: Adapt and sustain

Refine the intervention based on findings. Continue monitoring and share results with patients and staff.

A practical measurement set may include:
- **Outcome indicator:** The patient experience the team wants to improve.
- **Process indicator:** Whether the intended care process is being implemented.
- **Balancing indicator:** Whether the change creates unintended problems elsewhere.

## 8. Data Quality and Ethical Considerations

Before interpreting results, check that:

- Indicator definitions have not changed without documentation.
- Numerators and denominators are calculated consistently.
- Missing responses are reported transparently.
- Survey methods are accessible to patients with different communication needs.
- Participation is voluntary and does not affect access to care.
- Patients can provide feedback without fear of negative consequences.
- Personal information is collected only when necessary and handled securely.
- Findings are not used to unfairly blame individual staff.
- Patient comments are anonymised before wider circulation where appropriate.
- Small samples and differences between patient groups are interpreted cautiously.

Patient feedback may reflect selection bias, recall bias, expectations, and the circumstances in which feedback was collected. These limitations should be acknowledged in reports.

## 9. Indicator Review and Revision

Review each indicator periodically and ask:

1. Does it measure something important to patients?
2. Is the definition clear and reproducible?
3. Are the data sufficiently complete and trustworthy?
4. Does the indicator reveal differences between patient groups?
5. Has it helped the team make or evaluate a change?
6. Is it still useful, or should it be revised or retired?

Avoid collecting indicators simply because the data are easy to obtain. Prioritise measures that support meaningful learning and improvement.

## 11. Key Principle

Patient voice indicators are not an end in themselves. Their value lies in whether healthcare organisations listen to patients, understand their experiences, act on what they learn, and determine whether those actions improve care.

**Measure what matters to patients, use the findings responsibly, and close the feedback loop by communicating what has changed.**
